Listed disease

African swine fever

Responsible for massive losses in pig populations and drastic economic consequences, African swine fever (ASF) has become a major crisis for the pork industry in recent years. It now affects several regions of the world, threatening animal health, biodiversity and the livelihoods of pig farmers.

What is African swine fever?

African swine fever is a highly contagious viral disease of domestic and wild pigs, whose mortality rate can reach 100%. It is not related to classical swine fever (hog cholera), although the signs can look similar.

ASF is not a risk to human health, but it devastates pig populations and farming economies. The virus is highly resistant in the environment: it can survive on clothes, boots, wheels and other materials, and in pork products such as ham, sausages and bacon.

Socio-economic impact

In many countries, pigs are a main source of household income. The spread of ASF has devastated family-run pig farms, often the backbone of people’s livelihoods. With pork making up more than a third of the meat eaten worldwide, ASF is also a food security concern. It threatens wild boar populations and the balance of ecosystems as well.

Global situation

ASF has spread beyond Africa to Europe, Asia, the Pacific and the Caribbean, affecting both domestic and wild pigs.

  • 83countries have reported ASF since 2005 (as of January 2025)
  • 12countries reported their first ASF cases since January 2022
  • 11countries reported spread to new zones since January 2022
  1. 2005Present in 32 countries in Africa
  2. 2007Appears in Eastern Europe (Georgia)
  3. 2014Reaches the European Union
  4. 2018Emerges in Asia (China)
  5. 2019–20Spreads to Oceania (Timor-Leste, Papua New Guinea)
  6. 2021Reappears in the Americas (Dominican Republic, Haiti)
  7. 2022–24Further spread across Africa, Asia and Europe

4 key measures

Prevention

Biosecurity is the most effective way to keep ASF out. See the 4 key measures for borders, farms, everyone, and early reporting.

4 key measures

Prevention

Keeping ASF out of free areas depends on the right import policies and biosecurity, so that neither infected live pigs nor pork products are brought in. Biosecurity is the most important and effective measure to prevent and control ASF:

  1. At borders and portsproperly treat food waste from aircraft, ships and vehicles, and stop illegal imports of pigs and pork products.
  2. On farmscontrol who and what enters, clean and disinfect boots, vehicles and equipment, and keep pigs away from wild pigs.
  3. For everyonedo not carry pork products from affected areas, stay away from pig farms and wild pigs, and never feed pigs food waste containing meat.
  4. Report earlytell your municipal or city veterinarian or this office right away about sick or dead pigs.

Control measures

When an outbreak occurs, the following measures are put in place:

  • Early detection, and humane killing of infected animals with proper disposal of carcasses.
  • Thorough cleaning and disinfection.
  • Zoning and controls on the movement of pigs and pork products.
  • Surveillance and epidemiological investigation.
  • Strict biosecurity measures on farms.

Where wild pigs help spread the disease, managing their numbers and their contact with backyard and low-biosecurity farms is key. Despite its challenges, ASF can be controlled with sustained effort and collaboration at local, national and international levels.

Frequently asked questions

What are the signs of ASF?

In the acute form: high fever, depression, loss of appetite and bleeding (red or purple patches) in the skin, with death within 6 to 20 days; mortality can reach 100%. Milder, longer-lasting forms cause weight loss, breathing problems and swollen joints, with 30 to 70% of pigs dying.

Where does ASF come from?

ASF was traditionally found in Africa. It reached Sardinia (Italy) in 1978 and Georgia in 2007, the European Union in 2014, Asia in 2018, Oceania in 2019, and reappeared in the Americas in 2021.

How does ASF spread?

Through direct contact between infected pigs, domestic or wild; through eating material contaminated with the virus, such as pork scraps or food waste; and through contaminated objects like clothes, boots, vehicles and equipment.

How is ASF diagnosed?

ASF may be suspected from the signs, but it must be confirmed by laboratory tests, which also rule out classical swine fever.

Can people get ASF? Is pork safe to eat?

ASF is not a risk to human health. People do not get sick from ASF, either by handling pigs or by eating pork. However, pork and food waste from infected pigs must never be fed to other pigs.

Must ASF be reported?

Yes. ASF must be reported to the veterinary authorities as soon as it is suspected. Contact your municipal or city veterinarian or this office right away.

How can we prevent ASF?

Biosecurity is the most effective measure: strictly apply farm biosecurity, avoid swill feeding, control the movement of pigs and pork products, and stay vigilant at borders and checkpoints.

Can ASF be controlled?

Yes. Despite its complexity, ASF can be controlled with sustained effort and collaboration from farmers, local governments, veterinary services and national and international partners.

Listed disease

Avian Influenza

With devastating consequences for the poultry industry, farmers’ livelihoods, trade and the health of wild birds, avian influenza, also known as “bird flu”, has drawn the attention of the international community for years. Migratory waterfowl are its natural reservoir, and people are occasionally infected when the virus circulates in poultry.

What is avian influenza?

Avian influenza is a highly contagious viral disease that affects both domestic and wild birds. It is caused by influenza type A viruses, which are divided into many subtypes, such as H5N1, H5N3 and H5N8. Depending on how severe the disease is in poultry, the viruses are grouped into two types:

  • LPAILow pathogenicity avian influenza: usually causes few or no clinical signs in birds.
  • HPAIHigh pathogenicity avian influenza: causes severe signs and can kill many birds quickly.

Transmission and spread

Infected birds shed the virus in their droppings and respiratory secretions. Birds catch it through close contact with these secretions, especially droppings, or through contaminated feed and water. The virus survives well in the environment, so it can also move from farm to farm on equipment, vehicles, crates, clothes and shoes. Three main factors spread the disease:

  1. Movement of infected birdsincluding trade in live birds between farms, markets and provinces.
  2. Farming practices and live bird marketswhere many birds from different sources are kept close together.
  3. Wild birds and migratory routeswhich can carry the virus over long distances and bring it into new areas.

Wild water birds have long carried low pathogenicity viruses without getting sick. Recently, however, high pathogenicity strains have also infected many wild bird species, spreading along migratory routes and killing even endangered birds.

The impact of avian influenza

Avian influenza can wipe out entire flocks, causing heavy losses for farmers and the whole poultry sector.

  • ~50%Mortality in affected flocks is often around half of the birds, sometimes more.
  • JobsPoultry farming employs many people, so outbreaks can mean serious job and income losses.
  • CullingHealthy birds are often culled to contain outbreaks, wasting food and hurting farm income.
  • TradeOutbreaks lead to restrictions on moving and selling live birds and poultry meat.

The disease also harms wild birds, including endangered species, and can cross the species barrier to infect mammals, on land and at sea.

Public health risk

Bird-to-human transmission is rare and usually happens in a specific setting: people in close and repeated contact with infected birds or heavily contaminated environments are most at risk. Because several subtypes keep circulating, avian influenza remains a public health concern around the world. There is no evidence that properly cooked poultry meat or eggs can pass the virus to people.

Biosecurity tips

Prevention and control

Early detection, biosecurity tips for poultry raisers, and what happens when infection is found.

The first line of defence is early detection. Report sick or dying poultry and wild birds right away to your municipal or city veterinarian or to this office.

Because the virus is hardy and highly contagious, strict biosecurity and good hygiene are essential:

  • Keep poultry away from wild birds, and their feed and water covered.
  • Keep poultry houses, cages and equipment clean, and control who and what enters the farm.
  • Do not bring in birds whose health status is unknown.
  • Dispose of manure, litter and dead birds properly.

When infection is found, infected birds and those in close contact are usually culled to contain the outbreak quickly, together with movement restrictions, better hygiene and biosecurity, and surveillance. Vaccination may be used in some situations, but only as part of a wider control strategy, never on its own.

A One Health approach

Because of its risk to human health and its far-reaching effects on wild bird populations, avian influenza must be tackled under a One Health approach, with animal health, human health and environmental sectors working together.

Frequently asked questions

Is avian influenza seasonal?

Its patterns are complex and hard to predict. Globally, outbreaks are often lowest around September, begin to rise in October and peak around February, influenced by wild bird migration, trade, farming systems, biosecurity and immunity.

What helps the virus spread locally?

The virus surviving in the environment, movement of infected birds, contaminated equipment, the movement of wild birds, and mixing of wild birds from different places.

Can people catch avian influenza?

Rarely. It usually happens only after close contact with infected birds or heavily contaminated places. Culled birds must never enter the food or animal feed chain.

Is it safe to eat chicken, duck and eggs?

There is no evidence that eating poultry meat or eggs can pass on the virus. Always cook poultry and eggs thoroughly and practise good kitchen hygiene.

What should farmers do to protect their flocks?

Prevent contact between poultry and wild birds; limit movement around poultry enclosures; strictly control vehicles, people and equipment entering the farm; avoid bringing in birds of unknown health status; dispose of manure, litter and dead birds properly; vaccinate where recommended; and report any suspicious illness or deaths to your municipal or city veterinarian or this office right away.

Must avian influenza be reported?

Yes. As soon as it is suspected or detected, avian influenza must be reported to the veterinary authorities under national rules.

Can cats catch avian influenza?

Occasionally. Cats can be infected through contact with infected birds, other animals or contaminated food such as raw poultry or unpasteurised milk. Signs include listlessness, loss of appetite, fever, difficulty breathing and neurological signs, and some cats have died. The risk of a sick cat passing the virus to a person is very low.

How can I protect my cat?

Keep cats away from sick poultry, dead wild birds and bird droppings; do not feed them raw poultry; keep outdoor shoes away from them and wash your hands regularly. If your cat may have been exposed and seems unwell, keep it apart from other pets and contact a veterinarian.

What should you do if your dog comes into contact with another dog with rabies?

Your dog is considered an exposed dog or close contact if it lives in the same household as, has mingled with, or was bitten by a dog confirmed to have rabies. What to do next depends on whether your dog is vaccinated.

If your dog is vaccinated

  1. Check the vaccination card. If the last rabies shot was more than a year ago, have your dog vaccinated right away.
  2. Watch your dog for any change in behavior over the next 3 months.
  3. Keep your dog away from other animals and people for now, but keep giving it food and everything else it needs.

If your dog is not vaccinated

  1. The rabies manual states that an unvaccinated exposed animal must be euthanized.
  2. If the owner does not agree, the dog may instead be vaccinated immediately and placed under quarantine for the next 6 months.
  3. Watch your dog for any change in behavior. Keep it away from other animals and people, but keep giving it food and everything else it needs.

Why exposed dogs need to be kept apart

  • Rabies is fatal.
  • It protects other animals from catching rabies.
  • It limits the animals and people your dog comes into contact with.

Listed disease

Rabies

With a fatality rate of almost 100% in humans and animals alike, rabies remains a global threat, killing approximately 59,000 people every year. Dogs are the main reservoir of the disease. Controlling and eliminating this deadly zoonosis therefore means combatting it at its animal source.

What is rabies?

Rabies is a viral disease that affects the central nervous system of mammals (dogs, cats, foxes and others), including humans. The virus is found mainly in the saliva and brain of infected animals, most commonly dogs, and is passed on by a bite. Because it can pass between animals and humans, rabies is a zoonotic disease (a zoonosis).

Early signs may be nonspecific: lethargy, fever, vomiting and loss of appetite. Within days they can progress to weakness and paralysis, loss of coordination, difficulty breathing and swallowing, excessive salivation, abnormal behaviour, aggression and/or self-mutilation. The incubation period can range from several weeks to several months, but once symptoms appear the disease is always fatal, in animals and in humans.

One of the deadliest zoonoses

For over 4,000 years rabies has affected almost every corner of the world. It has been eliminated in Western Europe, North America, Japan, South Korea and parts of Latin America, but it is still present in large parts of Africa and Asia. Most deaths, in humans and animals, are due to poor access to public health resources and preventive treatment, so low-income communities are hit hardest.

  • 99%of human rabies cases are due to bites from infected dogs
  • >95%of rabies deaths occur in Africa and Asia
  • >80%of cases occur in rural areas with little or no access to health education and post-bite treatment
  • 4 in 10rabies deaths are in children

Dog-mediated rabies elimination is possible

Unlike many other diseases, the tools needed to eliminate dog-mediated rabies already exist. Rabies is 100% preventable, and vaccinating dogs can eliminate the disease at its animal source.

Dog-mediated rabies is targeted for elimination by 2030. The Philippines is one of the few countries whose official control programme for dog-mediated rabies has been endorsed by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), alongside Malawi, Namibia, Saudi Arabia, Tanzania, Zambia and Zimbabwe.

4 key parts

Rabies control programmes

The 4 key parts of a successful programme against dog-mediated rabies, from surveillance to mass dog vaccination.

4 key parts

Rabies control programmes

Where rabies is endemic, measures are needed to lower the risk of infection in vulnerable animal populations (stray, domestic and wild), creating a barrier between the animal source and people. The key parts of a successful programme against dog-mediated rabies are:

  1. Surveillance and reportingto follow disease trends and detect new cases as early as possible.
  2. Mass dog vaccinationto tackle the disease at its source. Vaccinating at least 70% of dogs in at-risk areas can bring human cases down to zero.
  3. Dog population managementto reach a rabies-immune or rabies-free dog population while respecting animal welfare.
  4. Public awareness and educationso people understand the risks of rabies and how to prevent them.

These measures work alongside access to medical care and post-bite treatment for people, so close collaboration with human health authorities, under a One Health approach, is essential.

Frequently asked questions

How is rabies transmitted?

Through the saliva of an infected animal, mainly via bite wounds. About 99% of human cases are due to bites from infected dogs.

How does the virus spread in the body?

It usually stays at the entry site (such as a bite wound) for a while, then travels along the nerves to the brain, where it multiplies, and then moves along nerves to the salivary glands.

What is the incubation period?

From several days to several months, depending on the virus strain, the species, the individual animal and where the virus entered the body. An infected animal can sometimes pass on the disease through its saliva even before it shows signs.

What are the signs of rabies in animals?

Classically, sudden changes in behaviour: animals may lose their natural fear and come unusually close to people. As the disease progresses it causes loss of coordination, weakness, progressive paralysis, seizures, difficulty breathing and swallowing, excessive salivation, aggression and self-mutilation, and leads to death. Some animals show few changes and die quickly.

How is rabies diagnosed?

Rabies may be suspected from clinical signs, but only laboratory tests can confirm it. Samples from dead animals must be sent to a competent laboratory.

What should you do if an animal bites you?

Any bite by a domestic or wild animal must be investigated. Wash the wound immediately and thoroughly with soap and water for at least 15 minutes, see a doctor or go to the nearest animal bite treatment centre quickly, and report the bite to your municipal or city veterinarian or to this office.

Why vaccinate dogs?

Vaccinating dogs is the preferred way to control and eliminate rabies. Every successful elimination campaign has combined vaccination of all owned dogs with vaccination and management of stray dog populations. Culling animals is not considered a priority for control.

Can rabies be eliminated?

Yes. It has been estimated that just 10% of the money now spent on emergency treatment of people bitten by rabid dogs would be enough for veterinary services worldwide to eliminate rabies at its source, and so prevent almost all human cases.
